Case study 06 · Part II · Analytics

Reports & Analytics

Every month the MIS exports one file: around 800,000 rows by 90 columns, every member and every loan. Excel could barely hold it, and a single question meant a day of filtering, lookups and copying. For twenty-seven months I answered those questions with pandas instead. Regulators wanted classification schedules; finance wanted a bank-ready disbursement statement; programme heads wanted to know which products and activities carried the overdue; donors wanted sector splits. The answers are 73 notebooks — each one run in minutes and footed to the source, kept as they were written, checks included — and, later, a small library that defines each of the recurring numbers once.

The problem

The institution's MIS exports a monthly CM Report: one row per member-loan, around 800,000 rows by 90 columns, most of them headed in Bangla. It is the only complete picture of the loan book, and there was no reporting layer on top of it. Every question — from the regulator's half-yearly return to "which branches disburse most in the last three days of the month?" — meant someone working the file by hand.

Excel is the wrong tool at that size. The file sits near the row ceiling, opens slowly if at all, and a pivot or lookup across it can freeze the machine. Each answer took a day or more of filtering, lookups and copying between workbooks, and a wrong cell was invisible until someone downstream noticed.

The file fights back in smaller ways too. Dates arrive in four formats depending on which tool touched the export last. Member IDs and phone numbers lose their leading zeros the moment Excel opens them. Headers carry stray whitespace. A member with three loans appears on three rows, so a naive count triples them. And the fiscal year runs July to June, so "2023-2024" has to be derived, never read.

The detail I would defend in an interview

The checks stay in the code.

Scattered through the notebooks are lines like file['compulsory_withdrawn'].sum() - (rsp_w_returned.sum() + rsp_m_returned.sum()) — an expression whose only correct value is zero — and df[df['member_id'].duplicated(keep=False)], a look at exactly the rows that would double-count if forgotten. Many are commented out; they were run, satisfied, and left in place.

When these notebooks went into version control I was tempted to tidy them away. I didn't. They are the record of how each number was verified before it went to a regulator or a finance team, and they are the difference between "the total is 17,962" and "the total is 17,962 and here is why I believe it." A clean notebook with no checks is not more professional; it is less auditable.

What did change The definitions that every notebook re-implemented — the four-format date parser, the July–June fiscal year, total savings, PAR, the MRA and MAPLE classification bands, the activity-to-sector taxonomy, the split-into-workbooks-with-subtotals step — now live once, in a small library with tests against a synthetic export. New analysis imports them. Old analysis stands as written.

Two more decisions

  • Organised by question, not by who asked. The working folders were named after requesters. The repository is grouped into the seven programmes above, so a reader finds every PAR analysis in one place regardless of which manager commissioned it.
  • Sanitised by pipeline, not by hand. 104 notebooks carried rendered outputs with member data, absolute paths from two laptops, a database connection string and identifiers in notes. A scrubber clears outputs, rewrites paths to two environment-driven folders, replaces the connection string with an environment variable and redacts identifiers — and runs as a pre-commit check so nothing regresses. The same scrubber turned a 32 MB folder of private output into a repository that can be shown.

Capabilities

  1. Read the product name as data. One regular expression over the loan component string — JAGORON MONTHLY 1Y (13.5%) [Kisti/1K: 95] — yields four columns: instalment type, term, service-charge rate and instalment per thousand. Every product-wise cut starts there.
  2. Dates that are never re-read. Five date formats tried in order, each filling only what the earlier ones missed, Excel serials as the last resort. The July–June fiscal year is derived from the disbursement date, never read from a column.
  3. Members, not rows. A member with three loans is three rows. Savings are summed per member and broadcast back, borrowers are counted unique, and PAR is overdue borrowers' principal over total principal — one definition, tested.
  4. Regulatory schedules as data. Nine classification schemes — MRA loan and savings size, MAPLE term and aging, dormancy, disbursement range, lakh bands, borrower age — held as band edges, so a half-yearly return is one grouped pass per branch, footed to totals.
  5. Two months side by side. Columns prefixed by month and joined on zone, branch and member ID. Disbursed in December and overdue in January; overdue members given a new loan; NID verification in June against October — each is a filter on the join.
  6. Measures the export never carried. Instalments overdue from overdue ÷ instalment amount, days since the last instalment, borrower age from date of birth, expected repayment by instalment type and term, the share of a month's disbursement made in its last three days, transactions posted after 6 pm.
  7. Free text into sectors. Over a hundred income-generating-activity strings, some with Bangla in brackets, mapped to Agriculture, Processing & Manufacturing and Trading, and within agriculture to Livestock, Fisheries and Crops. Fifteen product prefixes map to programme families, most specific first. Anything unknown lands in Other rather than being guessed.
  8. Checked against the system of record. A parameterised SQLAlchemy query into the MIS database confirms each overdue account has a payment history behind it. Verified and unverified phone and NID populations were compared on outstanding and overdue before overdue tracing moved to a new system.
  9. Delivered in the shape people use. Subtotal rows per group, one workbook per zone or branch, frozen headers, autofilter and data validation through xlsxwriter. Pies for programme heads in matplotlib and plotly, a Power BI Python visual, and an ipywidgets front end so the MIS team can paste a path, press run and get the check.
  10. Reproducible without the data. A shared library with ten tests run against a seeded synthetic export. A scrubber clears outputs, rewrites paths, swaps credentials for environment variables and redacts member IDs; pre-commit runs it with nbstripout and gitleaks, so the repository can be shown without showing a member.

Impact

  • Minutes, not days. A question that took a day in a spreadsheet is a notebook run, and next month it is the same run on the next export.
  • Answers that foot. Every schedule reconciles to the raw export before it leaves. The check is in the code, not in someone's memory.
  • One definition per number. PAR, fiscal year, total savings and the nine classification bands are defined once, in a library with tests, so two reports can no longer disagree.
  • Recurring deliverables, owned. The regulator's half-yearly return, finance's monthly bank statement, the zone overdue packs and the member-count cross-checks all run from the same engine.
  • Twenty-seven months of regulator, finance, programme and donor questions answered from one dataset. 73 analyses kept in seven programmes, runnable by the next person.
  • The groundwork for Part I. The target-allocation engine, the reporting dashboard and the special-permission tracker were built on the understanding of the data these notebooks produced.
